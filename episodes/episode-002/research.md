# Episode 002 research notes

Verified from the paper text (read 2026-11-23):
- Ariely, Loewenstein & Prelec (2003), Coherent Arbitrariness, Quarterly Journal of Economics. Experiment 1: 55 MBA students at MIT Sloan. Subjects were asked whether they would pay a dollar figure equal to the last two digits of their social security number, then gave a maximum willingness to pay. Six products, average retail price about $70.
- Top vs bottom quintile of SS# numbers, mean stated value: cordless keyboard $55.64 vs $16.09; trackball $26.18 vs $8.64; Belgian chocolates $20.64 vs $9.55. The paper says top-quintile valuations were "typically greater by a factor of three."
- Script uses the keyboard example ("about fifty-six" vs "about sixteen").

Concept (not re-verified in this session): Tversky & Kahneman (1974), anchoring and insufficient adjustment. Script says only that judgements are adjusted from the starting point, "usually not enough".
