# Episode 001 research notes

Full ledger: `sources.json`. Summary of what is supported and what is not.

## Supported
- The left-digit effect is a documented, peer-reviewed finding (Thomas & Morwitz 2005): a price like $2.99 is encoded as nearer $2 than $3; the effect appears when the leftmost digit changes, and it faded with larger price gaps in their pen experiments.
- $9 endings increased demand in all three field experiments in Anderson & Simester (2003), more for new items; authors suggest limited information matters.
- Used-car prices step down at 10,000-mile thresholds, about $150 to $200 each, across 22 million+ wholesale transactions (Lacetera, Pope, Sydnor). Example: just under 80,000 miles sold ~$210 above just over; only ~$10 below 100 fewer miles.
- Strulov-Shlain: shoppers acted as if 1 cent were 15 to 25 cents while firms priced as if 1.5 to 3; one-quarter to one-third of prices ended in 99 cents in the data described.

## Not supported or disputed (not used as fact)
- Cash-register origin story: legend per Schindler coverage; no primary documentation found.
- 1875 Chicago Daily News and 1880 Macy's origin claims: unverified.
- "Over 60% of prices end in 9": unsourced radio claim.
- "$34 to $39 dress, sales up 30%+": unsourced; not in Anderson & Simester abstract.
- Whether luxury brands avoid .99 endings, or that .99 always lifts sales: no source checked, so not claimed.
