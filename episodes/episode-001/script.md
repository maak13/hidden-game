# Episode 001: Why a $9.99 price feels different from $10

**Series:** C, The Mind's Loopholes (with a Price of Everything crossover)

**Format:** 16:9 master (see open questions in brief.md). Method: Surface-to-System.
**Central question:** Why does one cent make a price feel so much cheaper?

## Scene 1 | Cold open | 0:00 to 0:12

**NARRATION**
Two price tags. One says nine ninety-nine. The other says ten dollars. The difference is one cent. So why do they feel so far apart?

## Scene 2 | Stakes | 0:12 to 0:31

**NARRATION**
Walk through any store and the nine is everywhere. In the data behind one economics study of a US retailer, roughly a quarter to a third of all prices ended in ninety-nine cents. That isn't habit. It's a bet on how your mind reads a number.

**VISUAL**
Grid of 12 to 18 small price tags fills the frame, mostly ending in .99. The .99 tags brighten gold one by one until about a third are lit, then the grid dims. Final beat: one tag stays lit and moves to centre.

**ON SCREEN**
Subtitle at bottom: "About 25 to 33% of prices in one retail dataset ended in .99" (source note, see sources S4).

**SOUND**
Soft rhythmic ticks as tags light up (sparse, not constant impacts).

## Scene 3 | Mechanism: the left-digit effect | 0:31 to 0:56

**NARRATION**
Here's the mechanism. We read prices left to right, and the first digit does most of the work. Researchers call it the left-digit effect. In 2005, Manoj Thomas and Vicki Morwitz found that two ninety-nine registers as closer to two dollars than to three. And it only kicks in when that first digit changes.

**VISUAL**
Surface-to-system transition. A $2.99 tag fills the screen. The leading "2" lifts out of the number, glows gold and grows; the "99" fades to grey. A number line from $2 to $3 appears under it, with a marker sitting visibly nearer $2 than $3. Then three tall glass cards slide up in a row, left to right: "First digit" > "Read as lower" > "Feels cheaper". Last beat: a $3.29 and $3.30 pair with the same leading digit, marked "same first digit, no jump".

**ON SCREEN**
Title: "The left-digit effect". Subtitle: "Thomas & Morwitz, Journal of Consumer Research, 2005". Glass card labels as listed (Title style, short).

**SOUND**
A single rising tone as the "2" lifts out, resolving as the cards land.

## Scene 4 | Evidence: cars | 0:56 to 1:22

**NARRATION**
It shows up where real money is on the line. In more than twenty-two million used-car sales, prices dropped at every ten-thousand-mile mark. A car just under eighty thousand miles sold for about two hundred ten dollars more than one just over. But only ten dollars less than a car with a hundred fewer miles. One digit mattered more than the wear.

**VISUAL**
Odometer close-up in the brand palette. It rolls 79,800 > 79,900 > 79,999 > 80,000 while a gold price bar to the right shifts. Show three vertical glass bars side by side labelled with mileage bands (79,800 to 79,899 / 79,900 to 79,999 / 80,000 to 80,100) with heights proportional to price, so the big drop between the last two and the tiny gap between the first two is obvious. Gold bracket and "$210" between bars 2 and 3; small grey bracket and "$10" between bars 1 and 2.

**ON SCREEN**
Title: "$210 vs $10". Subtitle: "22 million used-car sales. Lacetera, Pope & Sydnor (NBER working paper)".

**SOUND**
Mechanical odometer click-roll, slowing into the 80,000 rollover.

## Scene 5 | Complication and twist: stores underprice the effect | 1:22 to 1:42

**NARRATION**
Retail tells the same story. In field experiments, nine endings raised demand every time, especially for new items. And across seventeen hundred products, shoppers reacted as if that one cent were worth fifteen to twenty-five. Stores priced as if it were worth two or three.

**VISUAL**
Two tall glass columns rise side by side from a baseline. Left column (gold) grows to "15 to 25 cents" labelled "How shoppers reacted". Right column (grey) stops short at "about 2 to 3 cents" labelled "How stores priced". A thin "1 cent" marker line sits at the very bottom for scale.

**ON SCREEN**
Title: "1 cent". Subtitle: "Strulov-Shlain, Review of Economic Studies, 2023 (1,710 products)".

**SOUND**
Low swell as the left column rises past the right.

## Theories and quotes used (with confidence)

| # | Theory | Who | How we use it | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Left-digit effect:** prices are encoded as magnitudes, so $2.99 registers nearer $2 than $3. Effect appears when the leftmost digit changes; weaker or absent otherwise. | Thomas & Morwitz, 2005 | Scene 3 mechanism | High (peer reviewed; summarised via NYU Stern article) |
| 2 | **Limited information:** $9 endings may work better when shoppers have little information (new items vs items sold in earlier years). | Anderson & Simester, 2003 | Scene 5 | High for "increased demand in all three experiments"; medium for the mechanism, which the authors frame as a possibility |
| 3 | **Inattention to lower digits in a big-money market:** buyers underweight digits after the first. | Lacetera, Pope & Sydnor | Scene 4 | High for the price pattern; the authors attribute it mainly to final buyers |
| 4 | **Firms underestimate the bias:** shoppers behaved as if a 1 cent gap were 15 to 25 cents; firms priced as if 1.5 to 3. | Strulov-Shlain | Scene 5 twist | Medium to high (figures from Chicago Booth Review summary of the earlier working paper; confirm against the published paper) |
| 5 | **Cash-register origin story:** 9 endings made clerks open the register. | Reported as legend by Schindler | Scene 6 as a labelled legend only | Disputed. Do not present as fact |
| 6 | **Other origin claims** (an 1880 Macy's ad; 1875 Chicago Daily News penny shortage) | Various | Not used | Unverified. Leave out unless a primary source is found |

**Quotable lines (verify exact wording against the source before putting on screen):**
- Strulov-Shlain (via Chicago Booth Review): customers behaved "as if the price difference were 15 to 25 cents", while firms reacted as if it were "1.5 to 3 cents".
- Schindler (Fordham news story), on the cash register explanation: it is a "legend".

## Caveats and things we deliberately did not claim
- We do **not** say $9.99 always raises sales. Evidence shows it often increases demand, and that effect varies with the product and what shoppers know.
- We do **not** use the "$34 vs $39 dress, sales up over 30%" example. It appears only on an unsourced radio page, not in the Anderson & Simester source we checked.
- We do **not** claim that 60 percent of prices end in 9 (an unsourced radio figure). Only the 25 to 33 percent figure from the Strulov-Shlain dataset is used.
- Prices in different currencies and countries vary; this video describes US-based studies.
- Illustrative numbers (like $3.29 vs $3.30) are our examples, consistent with the left-digit hypothesis, not results from a study.
